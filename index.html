Joint Learning Agenda for Anticipatory Action

A shared framework for coordinated evidence generation, aligning the pace of learning with the pace of implementation, 2026–2030.

1Why a joint agenda now

Anticipatory action rests on a simple and powerful idea: acting ahead of a predictable shock can protect assets, lives, and livelihoods more effectively than acting later. Because it is built on decisions agreed in advance (when to act, with what, and for whom), it is an area where evidence can directly shape how resources are used. Rigorous evidence helps design better programmes, direct resources to where they yield the greatest impact, and give governments, donors and implementers the confidence to invest early and at scale.

Anticipatory action already benefits from a strong evidence base. Over the past decade, a substantial and growing body of rigorous studies across geographies and hazards has consistently shown that reaching people before a shock can protect food security, reduce distress sales and harmful coping, and preserve productive assets. This agenda does not start from scratch: it builds on these efforts as its foundation and seeks to connect and extend them rather than discard or replace them.

With the case for acting early well established, the frontier has moved to making anticipatory action work better. Which design features matter most? How can interventions be structured so that short-term protection leads to lasting outcomes? And how do their costs compare with those of conventional response, or of not acting at all? These are comparative questions that no single study can settle; answering them requires moving from individual studies to a shared effort.

Generating this evidence faces two kinds of obstacles. The first is operational: short lead times between a trigger and a shock, fragile and insecure settings, the cost of collecting timely, good-quality data, and the need to coordinate evaluation with the delivery of interventions in the field. The second is fragmentation: agencies use different indicators, cost measures, and analytical frameworks, so results can rarely be compared or aggregated across studies. Each study may be sound on its own, yet its findings remain tied to the context in which it was conducted, and evidence that travels across geographies and projects is worth far more than results that cannot. With funding tightening and anticipatory action still representing a small share of humanitarian spending, every evaluation needs to contribute to a shared body of knowledge.

2What the agenda is, who it is for, and what it adds

The Joint Learning Agenda (JLAA) is a shared framework, not a platform or a fund. It provides an overarching structure under which the efforts of individual institutions and practitioners are systematised, so that each contribution is magnified by being comparable with, and cumulative to, the work of others. Partners continue to design, fund and implement their own studies; the agenda aligns them around common priorities, shared measures and a joint commitment to put the results to use.

A focused scope

The agenda concentrates on evidence about the impact, effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of anticipatory action for the people it aims to protect.

Who the agenda is for

The agenda is open to any organisation that generates, finances, or uses evidence on anticipatory action and is willing to work towards its shared priorities and principles. Participating partners are expected to include UN agencies implementing anticipatory action; the Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement; NGOs and their networks; research institutions, universities, and multilateral development banks with evaluation capacity; donors and funds that finance anticipatory action or its evaluation; and national governments and disaster risk management authorities that lead anticipatory action in their countries. Beyond its signatories, the agenda is designed for those who make decisions about anticipatory action: programme designers deciding what to deliver, when, and to whom; donors and fund managers allocating pre-arranged financing; and governments seeking to scale anticipatory action through their own systems. Ultimately, it serves the communities that anticipatory action aims to protect, whose wellbeing is the measure of whether the evidence leads to better action.

Value for participating partners

Partners who sign on gain early visibility of what and where others are evaluating, opportunities to co-design studies and share baselines, a stronger position in mobilising resources through joint proposals, and findings that carry more weight because they are comparable across contexts. In return, partners contribute their studies to the shared portfolio, apply the common measurement module where feasible, share data under agreed protocols, and take part in defining shared priorities. Participation is deliberately light: it runs through the existing Anticipatory Action MEAL Working Group, relying on regular exchanges and a shared inventory of studies rather than on new structures, as described in Section 7.

3Agenda objectives

The agenda pursues four objectives:

A roadmap for evidence building

An overarching framework across partners that builds on and complements existing work rather than duplicating it.

A shared reference for tracking progress and setting expectations

A common view of what has been learned, what remains open, and what anticipatory action can and cannot achieve. Clarifying these boundaries helps the community prioritise the studies that yield the greatest learning and helps programme designers set objectives that anticipatory action can realistically meet.

A bridge from evidence to action

A commitment to put both existing and new findings to work in programme design, financing decisions, and policy, so that learning translates into better anticipatory action.

A lever for mobilising resources

A way for partners to pool effort and use each other’s commitments to attract further funding, without necessarily pooling budgets.

4Knowledge gaps and priority questions

The FAO workshop “Leveraging Evidence for Effective Anticipatory Action” (Rome, April 2026), which brought together more than 150 researchers, practitioners, and donors, provided an important starting point for identifying knowledge gaps. Since then, these have been further informed and refined through discussions at the WFP Global Impact Evaluation Forum (Geneva, September 2026), the ISDC Roundtable on Evidence in Fragile Settings (Berlin, September 2026), and within the Anticipatory Action Task Force (AATF) and the Anticipatory Action MEAL Working Group. Building on these inputs, the following are proposed as an initial and evolving set of priority knowledge gaps, to be further refined and complemented through the learning agenda process.

Gap 1Conditions under which anticipatory action works best

Trigger timing, transfer size, modality, targeting, and messaging vary widely across programmes, but systematic comparison is scarce and evidence is concentrated on specific modalities and shocks.

  • How do the timing and sequencing of assistance affect outcomes for rapid- and slow-onset hazards?
  • How does cash compare with in-kind assistance, and what is the added value of combined interventions and of multi-sectoral packages?
  • Which design features drive differential outcomes across hazards and household types, and how do the two impact pathways operate in practice?

Gap 2Linkages with long-term outcomes and resilience

Most evaluations capture outcomes weeks or months after an activation. Whether the gains last, and how short-term protection connects to longer-term wellbeing, remains largely unknown.

  • Do the gains from anticipatory action persist through the season and across repeated shocks?
  • How do short-term protective effects translate into longer-term outcomes for households, such as health, nutrition, and education, particularly when anticipatory action is linked to resilience and disaster risk management programming?

Gap 3Cost-effectiveness and value for money

Cost data are fragmented and rarely comparable, which limits the confidence that underpins scaling and financing decisions.

  • What is the cost-effectiveness of anticipatory action relative to alternative emergency interventions and to inaction?
  • How can a common methodology capture both avoided losses and added benefits, and the full costs of readiness as well as delivery?

Gap 4Coverage

Studies currently cluster around specific geographies, modalities, and shocks. Across all the gaps above, the portfolio will therefore be built to diversify the evidence base deliberately, giving particular attention to multi-hazard settings and to under-represented regions and livelihood systems, so that findings can be compared and generalised.

5Principles and selection criteria

Decision-relevant

Each study starts from the decisions it is meant to inform, such as who to reach, when to act and with what, rather than from the method.

Build on what exists

Existing evidence and syntheses are used before new studies are commissioned, and available findings are put to work in programming.

Transparent, tiered rigour

Not every evaluation can meet the most rigorous standard, and setting the bar on method alone would concentrate evidence where methods are easiest to apply rather than where it is most needed. The agenda draws on multiple evaluation types, quantitative and qualitative, using the most rigorous methods where feasible and robust alternatives elsewhere, with each study’s approach communicated openly rather than pooled as if equivalent.

Comparable by design

Studies apply the common measurement module where feasible and document key contextual features, such as hazard type, lead time and livelihood system, so that findings can travel across settings.

Proportionate and ethical

Data collection is limited to what is needed, avoids over-surveying communities, and addresses the ethics of comparison groups explicitly. Communities are informed of findings and treated as partners, not only as respondents.

Studies will be considered part of the shared portfolio when they address one or more of the priority gaps, can establish a credible baseline or comparison before assistance is delivered, inform a concrete programme or financing decision, add diversity to the evidence base in terms of hazard, region or modality, and apply the common measurement module where feasible.

6Proposed actions

Generate a critical mass of coordinated evidence

At least 10 rigorous analytical studies by 2030, spanning different geographies, themes, and time horizons, and selected as a portfolio against the priorities above rather than as isolated projects.

A common measurement module

Partners will agree a small, shared set of core outcome indicators and a common approach to costing, drawing on tools already used by partner agencies, so that studies can be compared and pooled in meta-analysis.

A living inventory of evidence and ongoing studies

Building on existing platforms, notably the Anticipation Hub, partners will maintain a public-facing inventory of completed and ongoing anticipatory action evaluations, mapped by geography, hazard type, modality, and priority gap, so that gaps and opportunities for collaboration are visible early.

Coordinated, evaluation-ready studies

To make the most of limited resources, partners will seek joint evaluations with shared baselines and pre-defined outcomes and analyses. Where possible, evaluation protocols, ethical clearance, and survey instruments will be prepared alongside anticipatory action plans, so that studies can be deployed as soon as a trigger is reached instead of starting from zero each time.

Open data

Anonymised data from studies in the portfolio will be shared under agreed protocols, reducing redundant data collection and enabling pooled analysis.

From evidence to action

Findings will be timed and packaged for the decisions they are meant to inform: revisions of anticipatory action plans, allocation of pre-arranged financing, and programme design. This includes short, decision-oriented briefs, exchanges between countries and agencies facing similar choices, and feedback to the communities that took part in the studies.

Stakeholder engagement

Governments, donors, Member countries, and affected communities will be engaged both in shaping the questions and in using the answers, with dedicated briefings on how the agenda’s outputs inform scaling and financing decisions.

7Governance and financing

The agenda relies on light governance that builds on existing structures rather than creating new ones. Its governance is organised around four roles.

Participating stakeholders

Agencies, donors, governments, research institutions, and practitioners that sign on to the agenda collectively define the learning priorities, contribute studies and data, and endorse the agenda and its revisions by consensus. Participation is formalised through institutional endorsement by each partner, as described in Section 8.

Anticipatory Action MEAL Working Group (AAWG-MEAL)

The Working Group serves as an informal secretariat. It facilitates the process, coordinates inputs from participating stakeholders, maintains the agenda and the inventory of studies, and follows up on decisions.

Technical core group

A small group of technical specialists drawn from the Anticipatory Action MEAL Working Group, and open to research partners, carries the substantive work between meetings. It prepares proposals on learning priorities, develops the common measurement module, reviews which studies meet the selection criteria for the shared portfolio, and coordinates the synthesis of results. Membership is voluntary and can rotate to reflect the range of participating institutions. The group’s proposals are submitted to participating stakeholders for endorsement.

Anticipatory Action Task Force

The Task Force leads the advocacy dimension and the linkages with anticipatory action programming, translating the learning objectives and emerging findings both into relevant changes in anticipatory action programmes and into effective messages for donors and governments, so that learning is turned into action. A priority is to identify which messages are best substantiated by evidence, and to be candid about where evidence is still thin.

Financing

The agenda works on two tiers. Individual studies are financed by participating partners, either by a single partner through its own resources, or jointly by two or more partners who co-finance a study of shared interest. In both cases, partners can use each other’s commitments to leverage further funding and to work around the rigidities of individual funding mechanisms. A limited set of shared functions requires modest joint resources: the common measurement module, the inventory of studies, and the synthesis of results. Across both tiers, evidence generation needs funding that is pre-arranged, in the same spirit as anticipatory action itself, and additional to, rather than competing with, humanitarian programming budgets. This funding can come either through dedicated evidence windows within anticipatory action funding streams or through stand-alone research grants. Without a distinct funding track, evaluations will remain opportunistic rather than systematic.

Phases

  1. Living document · 2026The agenda is maintained as a living document, open for comment until the end of the year, so that every partner institution and donor can propose priorities. A working version, rather than a final text, will be presented at the Global Dialogue Platform in Berlin at the end of October.
  2. Validation and endorsement · late 2026 to early 2027Participating stakeholders validate the learning priorities and agree the common agenda. Each partner then secures endorsement of its participation at the appropriate institutional level. In parallel, the technical core group is formed, a first version of the common measurement module is drafted, and opportunities to embed evidence generation in upcoming activations are identified.
  3. Coordinated evidence generation · 2027 to 2030Partners launch coordinated evaluations under the common measurement module, with a mid-term stocktake in 2028 to review progress and adjust priorities as evidence accumulates.
  4. Synthesis and uptake · 2030Findings are synthesised through comparative and meta-analysis and communicated to governments, donors and Member countries to inform scaling and financing decisions.

Measures of success

Progress will be judged not only by the number of studies completed, but by how far the evidence base has become shared and used:

  • the number of organisations that have formally endorsed the agenda;
  • the number of studies in the portfolio, including joint evaluations with shared baselines;
  • the share of anticipatory action evaluations applying the common measurement module;
  • the completion of pooled analyses across countries and hazards; and
  • documented cases in which evidence generated under the agenda informed a programme design, scaling or financing decision.

How this agenda was developed

This agenda builds on the FAO workshop “Leveraging Evidence for Effective Anticipatory Action” (Rome, 20–21 April 2026), which brought together more than 50 speakers and 150 participants from UN agencies, governments, donors, research institutions and NGOs. It has since been informed by discussions at the WFP Global Impact Evaluation Forum (Geneva, September 2026), the ISDC roundtable on evidence in fragile settings (Berlin, September 2026), and bilateral consultations with partners. It remains a living document and will continue to evolve through the contributions of participating stakeholders.
